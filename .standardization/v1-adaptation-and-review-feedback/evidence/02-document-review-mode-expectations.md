# Document review-mode expectations and version 1 coverage

Research artifact for [Assess document review-mode expectations and v1 coverage](../tickets/02-assess-document-review-mode-expectations-and-v1-coverage.md).

Observed and accessed: 2026-08-30

## Bounded question and decision impact

For users reviewing text-focused documents, which user-visible outcomes recur across representative document-review systems; which differences from code-editor or pull-request review are material; and for each outcome does [Web Editor Revisions v1](../../../standards/v1/standard.md) cover the semantic requirement, deliberately exclude the concern, or leave the relationship ambiguous?

This evidence can change the aim, limitations, examples, evaluation method, or disposition of [Clarify user-important document review mode](../changes/02-clarify-user-important-document-review-mode.md). It does not select a normative UI or workflow requirement. “Important” below means either a directly observed product affordance or an inference supported by product convergence and human-factors evidence; it is not a prevalence claim.

## Scope, source classes, and stopping condition

The released baseline is the immutable `web-editor-revisions-v1` tag at commit `e6ac89287257646888a4eadf692d836eb8feb41b`, with the canonical artifact at `standards/v1/standard.md`. The review-mode comparison uses the baseline's accepted-state/proposal distinction, proposal lifecycle, resolution projections, target remapping, mapping persistence, and explicit exclusions in Sections 1, 3, 7–11, 14, 15, and 18.

Source classes sampled:

- primary product documentation for three materially different text-document review systems: Google Docs Suggesting, Microsoft Word Track Changes, and LibreOffice Writer Track Changes;
- primary product documentation for two code-review systems: GitHub pull-request reviews and GitLab merge-request reviews; and
- high-trust human-factors research: a peer-reviewed ACM Transactions on Computer-Human Interaction study of accessible collaborative writing, a Microsoft Research user study of annotation positioning under document modification, and a PACM HCI interview study of collaborative-writing practice.

The stopping condition in the ticket is met: three document systems and two code-review systems are covered; every outcome in the synthesis is either directly sourced or labeled as an inference; and the v1 coverage matrix is stable enough to support a disposition decision. Additional products would mostly add presentation variants unless the decision requires a direct user-preference study or a materially different review model.

The human-factors sources qualify this matrix rather than adding another product workflow. The ACM study evaluated 48 screen-reader users and found that collaboration awareness depends on where comments are, who commented or edited, and what changed; contextual before/after presentation helped users understand how a sentence evolved, while interleaved markup could create cognitive overload ([Das, Piper, and Gergle, 2022](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10346423), pp. 9:1–3, 9:7–9, 9:26–29). A Microsoft Research study reports two user studies on how people react when annotations are lost or repositioned after document changes ([Brush et al., 2000](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/robust-annotation-positioning-in-digital-documents/)); this is evidence about annotation anchoring, not a claim that comments are revision proposals. An interview study of 30 academic and industry participants found reluctance to fully commit to synchronous co-editing despite commercial track-changes capabilities ([Wang, Tan, and Lu, 2017](https://research.ibm.com/publications/why-users-do-not-want-to-write-together-when-they-are-writing-together-users-rationales-for-todays-collaborative-writing-practices)); product affordances therefore should not be treated as proof of a preferred workflow.

## Findings

### Direct facts from document-review systems

1. **A reviewer needs to distinguish base content from a proposed edit in reading context.** Google Docs keeps the original text unchanged while suggestions are pending, displays additions in a new color and deletions crossed out, and offers accepted/rejected previews. Word exposes inline or balloon markup and separate Original/No Markup/All Markup views. LibreOffice displays recorded insertions, deletions, and formatting changes in the text and selects a change from its management list. ([Google Docs](https://support.google.com/docs/answer/6033474?hl=en), [Word](https://support.microsoft.com/en-US/Word/training/track-changes-in-word), [LibreOffice](https://help.libreoffice.org/latest/en-US/text/shared/guide/redlining_enter.html))

2. **A reviewer needs an explicit decision operation.** Google documents one-by-one and all-suggestion accept/reject. Word documents single-change, sequential, all, and all-shown accept/reject. LibreOffice documents individual or all-together accept/reject, including selection from a list. These are product facts; the inference that per-proposal resolution is a cross-system minimum is strong for the sampled systems, while the exact button, queue, and grouping are not universal. ([Google Docs](https://support.google.com/docs/answer/6033474?hl=en), [Word](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/word/accept-or-reject-tracked-changes-in-word), [LibreOffice](https://help.libreoffice.org/latest/en-GB/text/shared/guide/redlining_accept.html))

3. **A reviewer needs to know what a decision will leave behind.** Google exposes accepted and rejected previews in its current API and its user help exposes a preview before all-suggestion resolution. Word distinguishes temporarily hiding markup from actually accepting or rejecting it: No Markup does not remove changes. LibreOffice describes producing a final document from accepted or ignored suggestions, but the cited help does not establish a universal preview-mode vocabulary. This supports a semantic before/after outcome, not a universal three-view UI. ([Google Docs API](https://developers.google.com/workspace/docs/api/how-tos/suggestions), [Google Docs help](https://support.google.com/docs/answer/6033474?hl=en), [Word](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/word/accept-or-reject-tracked-changes-in-word), [LibreOffice](https://help.libreoffice.org/latest/en-US/text/shared/guide/redlining.html))

4. **Attribution and triage are useful, but their exact form diverges.** Word provides author colors, reviewer filters, change-type filters, and navigation. LibreOffice records author/date and supports author/date filtering, hierarchical modified changes, and navigation. Google exposes suggestion IDs/status and threaded posts in its API, but the user help used here does not establish an equivalent author/date filtering queue. Thus “identify the change and its source when available” is supported; mandatory author/time metadata or a universal review queue is not. ([Word](https://support.microsoft.com/en-US/Word/training/track-changes-in-word), [LibreOffice](https://help.libreoffice.org/latest/en-GB/text/shared/guide/redlining_accept.html), [Google Docs API](https://developers.google.com/workspace/docs/api/how-tos/suggestions))

5. **Comments and discussion commonly accompany, but are not identical to, content changes.** Google permits a comment on a suggestion and replies; Word treats comments alongside tracked changes; LibreOffice permits comments on recorded changes. The systems do not establish that discussion itself is a mutation or that resolving a comment resolves a text proposal. ([Google Docs](https://support.google.com/docs/answer/6033474?hl=en), [Word comments and changes](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/word/track-changes-and-view-add-or-edit-comments), [LibreOffice recording changes](https://help.libreoffice.org/latest/en-US/text/shared/guide/redlining_enter.html))

6. **Safe target continuity matters when the underlying document changes, but evidence for comments is not evidence for proposal remapping.** The Microsoft Research annotation studies directly examined users' reaction to lost or incorrectly repositioned annotations after document modifications. The ACM study likewise found that screen-reader users need contextual information about how edits alter a sentence. These findings support making an attachment failure visible and contextual, but they do not establish a particular target algorithm or make annotation anchors part of a revision interchange contract. ([Brush et al.](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/robust-annotation-positioning-in-digital-documents/), [Das et al.](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10346423), pp. 9:7–9, 9:26–29)

### Direct facts from code-review systems

1. **GitHub review is patch- and line-oriented.** GitHub's official quickstart directs the reviewer to the Files changed view, line or file comments, optional suggestion blocks, and a review-level Comment, Approve, or Request changes decision. A later code-modifying commit can dismiss an approval when the repository's required-review settings enable stale-review dismissal. ([GitHub quickstart](https://docs.github.com/en/pull-requests/get-started/reviewing-pull-requests-quickstart), [GitHub proposed changes](https://docs.github.com/en/pull-requests/how-tos/review-pull-requests/reviewing-proposed-changes-in-a-pull-request))

2. **GitLab review is patch-, discussion-, and integration-oriented.** Its review guidance separates Overview, Commits, Pipelines, and Changes; asks reviewers to understand intent, inspect a diff, test, and check pipelines; and supports draft comments, thread resolution, re-review, and review-level Approve, Comment, or Request changes. Approval rules, open discussions, conflicts, and CI/CD status can affect mergeability. ([GitLab tutorial](https://docs.gitlab.com/tutorials/reviews/), [GitLab review workflow](https://docs.gitlab.com/user/project/merge_requests/reviews/), [GitLab approvals](https://docs.gitlab.com/user/project/merge_requests/approvals/))

3. **The code-review “decision” is not the same semantic object as accepting a document proposal.** This is an inference from the product contracts: GitHub and GitLab resolve a review or merge request against a branch/commit integration boundary; their suggestions are comments or author-applied patch edits. They do not expose the document-review pattern of resolving each insertion, deletion, replacement, or formatting proposal into an accepted document projection. Code review adds repository context, tests, mergeability, approval rules, and re-review after new commits. The overlap—diff context, targeted comments, identity, and a final review signal—does not justify importing code-review gates or terminology into the document core.

### Human-factors findings and contradictions

- The ACM study gives direct human-factors evidence for three user-relevant pieces of collaboration awareness: where the change/comment is, who acted, and what was changed. Its participants also benefited from hearing or reading original and modified sentence context, but richer markup, voice changes, and dense comments introduced distraction or cognitive load. The result supports contextual, task-selectable presentation as a design concern while contradicting any claim that “more markup” or one fixed view is universally better ([Das et al.](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10346423), pp. 9:7–9, 9:23–27, 9:29).
- The annotation-positioning research found that users can react badly to lost annotations and may sometimes prefer an annotation to be visibly orphaned over a plausible but wrong placement. This is a warning against silent relocation, not evidence that review comments and content proposals have the same lifecycle ([Brush et al.](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/robust-annotation-positioning-in-digital-documents/), pp. 1–2, 4–6).
- The collaborative-writing interview study found reluctance to fully commit to co-editing even though track-changes capabilities were widely available. This contradicts treating product convergence as a direct statement of user preference and limits the normative force of the product matrix ([Wang et al.](https://research.ibm.com/publications/why-users-do-not-want-to-write-together-when-they-are-writing-together-users-rationales-for-todays-collaborative-writing-practices)).

## What users likely mean by “review mode”

The following is an evidence-backed inference, not a proposed normative clause:

> In a text-focused document, review mode is a way to inspect pending changes in document context, understand the affected content and available attribution, decide which changes to keep or discard, preview the resulting reading state, and finish with an intentional clean or still-marked document. Comments, permissions, notifications, and workflow gates may surround this activity but are separate concerns.

The highest-confidence semantic outcomes in the sample are:

- distinguish pending/proposed content from the current accepted reading;
- inspect the changed content in enough surrounding context to understand its effect;
- make an explicit accept/reject decision for one change and, where supported, a compatible set or all changes;
- observe or derive the resulting accepted/rejected reading before or after committing; and
- retain correct attachment or expose an unresolvable attachment rather than silently attaching a review artifact to the wrong text.

Navigation, filtering, author/date attribution, comments, reviewer permissions, notification, and accessibility presentation are important product concerns, but the evidence shows divergence in their availability and design. They should not be treated as one universal “review mode” contract without a separate normative decision and broader user research.

## Assessment implications (without selecting the disposition)

1. V1 **does answer the portable semantic question** behind review mode: what pending proposals are, how their content and targets are represented, what accept/reject means, what projections result, how remaining targets are remapped, and how an adapter reports loss or refusal. This is the part that can be used by a UI, an import/export path, or an automated editing system.

2. V1 **does not answer the user-facing review-mode question** if that question means a required visual or interaction mode. It intentionally excludes editor UI, navigation, comments, permissions, workflow policy, and accessibility presentation. That is a visible limitation, not evidence that the core's proposal semantics are missing.

3. The code-review comparison is useful only as a boundary aid. Document review and code review share contextual change inspection, targeted feedback, reviewer identity, summary decisions, and re-review. Code review additionally centers on commits/branches, tests, mergeability, approval rules, and integration gates. A code-review `Approve` or `Request changes` status is not a document proposal's accepted/rejected projection, and a code suggestion block is not automatically a portable revision proposal.

4. The strongest evidence-backed maintenance clarification would be to state the boundary explicitly: v1 is an interchange and observable-resolution contract that can underpin a document review surface; it is not a universal review-mode UI or collaboration workflow. Whether that clarification belongs in informative guidance, limitations, examples, evaluation criteria, or a new profile is a normative disposition question for the linked change item.

## Supporting and contradictory evidence

Supporting evidence is the convergence of three document systems on pending-vs-current distinction, per-change decision, and a finalized outcome, plus the v1 baseline's independent definitions of accepted state, proposal lifecycle, exact projections, and remapping. The ACM accessibility study supports the user value of contextual before/after understanding and collaborator/change attribution. GitHub and GitLab support the comparison's claim that code review has a review-level integration decision and repository checks in addition to local change inspection.

Contradictory or qualifying evidence is material:

- Google, Word, and LibreOffice use different names, views, storage/persistence boundaries, grouping, filtering, and comment workflows. Google offers explicit accepted/rejected API previews; Word warns that No Markup only hides changes; LibreOffice foregrounds a hierarchy/filter/manage list. No one product establishes the universal UI.
- The ACM study found that adding collaboration markup can create cognitive overload and disrupt reading, even when it carries needed information. A single always-on markup presentation would therefore be a poor normative inference.
- The annotation studies concern comments/annotations, not mutation proposals; their warning about orphaning supports visible uncertainty and context but does not justify adding comment semantics to v1.
- The collaborative-writing interview study found reluctance to fully co-edit despite track-changes availability. Product affordances show what is possible, not what all users prefer.
- GitHub and GitLab documentation describes repository governance and merge checks that have no direct document-projection equivalent. Importing those gates would expand v1 into workflow policy, which the baseline explicitly excludes.

## Uncertainty, omissions, and source limitations

- Product help pages are authoritative for the documented current affordances but are not usability studies, adoption surveys, or evidence of a shared user preference. Google API documentation accessed here includes current Developer Preview features and access-dependent behavior; product surfaces can change.
- The human-factors evidence is high-trust but bounded: the ACM paper focuses on 48 screen-reader users; the Microsoft Research work is a 2000 technical report about annotation anchoring; and the collaborative-writing study is a qualitative interview study of 30 participants. None establishes a universal ranking of document-review outcomes.
- The sample does not include Apple Pages, OnlyOffice, Overleaf, Adobe Acrobat, legal redlining systems, or manuscript-review platforms. These may add workflow or layout variants; evidence that one of them changes the semantic v1 disposition would require a materially different proposal/resolution model, not just another UI style.
- No sampled source directly measures user preference for accepting formatting, paragraph split/merge, or atomic replacement; those outcomes are inferred from the v1 semantics and product capabilities, not claimed as universal user priorities.
- The code-review sample contains two repository products, not a census of code editors. It is sufficient for the ticket's comparison requirement but not for standardizing code-review behavior.

Evidence would change the assessment if a representative document system lacked explicit pending-vs-current distinction or per-change resolution, if direct user studies showed a materially different minimum outcome, if accessibility evaluation showed that exact before/after projections were insufficient without a shared presentation contract, or if live adapters could not preserve the v1 projection/remapping semantics across the claimed boundary.

## Stopping rationale

The three document systems cover cloud suggestion threads, desktop/web track-change markup, and local list/hierarchy-based change management. The two code systems cover the patch/branch review and merge-gating comparison. Every synthesized outcome has either product documentation, human-factors support, or an explicit inference label. The v1 matrix distinguishes semantic coverage from deliberate UI/workflow exclusions and identifies the only materially unresolved boundary: whether the maintenance effort should add explanatory/evaluation guidance or a new normative surface. Sampling another mainstream word processor is unlikely to change that boundary; direct user research would be warranted only if the pending decision is about preference rather than specification scope.

## Newly visible questions and justified follow-up recommendations

No new ticket is created in this bounded research task. The following follow-ups are recommendations for the orchestrator:

- **Decision follow-up:** decide whether Change Item 02 should be resolved as a core-preserving clarification (informative review-mode boundary plus projection/remapping evaluation examples), a profile/implementation-guidance addition, or a broader UI/review-profile effort. The evidence does not justify silently making UI, comments, accessibility, or approval workflow normative in the v1 core.
- **Task follow-up if clarification is accepted:** add an example or evaluation note that a review surface should expose the accepted reading and proposal-specific acceptance/rejection projections, preserve proposal identity/attachment, and surface unsupported or unmappable cases. Keep the UI form implementation-defined.
- **Research follow-up only if the decision requires preference evidence:** commission direct observation/interviews with text-document reviewers, including screen-reader users, focused on the minimum useful review surface, batch-resolution expectations, and when comments/attribution become necessary. Product documentation alone cannot answer those preference questions.
